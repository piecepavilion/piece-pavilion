It’s-a Me, an Actual Minifigure: LEGO Super Mario Goes Minifig Scale

Piece Pavilion · September 8, 2026

Six years. That’s how long LEGO fans have been asking the same question: where are the Mario minifigures? The LEGO Super Mario line launched in 2020 with big interactive electronic figures that couldn’t sit on a shelf next to your Star Wars row, couldn’t swap a torso, and couldn’t ride in a normal LEGO car. Great toys. Not minifigures.

That just changed. At gamescom in late August, LEGO revealed eight brand-new minifigure-scale Super Mario sets — and with them the first true LEGO minifigures of Mario, Luigi, Princess Peach, Toad, Yoshi, Bowser Jr., Koopa Troopa and Goomba. They arrive January 1, 2027, priced from $9.99 to $99.99.

One housekeeping note, because we’ve seen this date garbled all over social: this is not an October drop. The reveal happened in August 2026; the sets go on sale January 1, 2027. Anything promising Mario minifigs on shelves this fall is mixing this wave up with 72051 Donkey Kong Arcade, the 1,367-piece $199.99 set that landed August 1 and does include one exclusive minifigure.

Here’s everything announced, with the figures in each box. Pay attention to that last column — it is the whole ballgame for valuing these later.

A few things jump out. Bowser isn’t a standard minifigure — he’s a new, larger figure that sits somewhere between a normal minifig and a classic big fig like Hulk or Thanos, on his own molds. There’s a brick-built cow in 72058, straight off Mario Kart’s farm track, which is exactly the kind of oddball that fans fall in love with. And Sidestepper — the crab from the original Mario Bros. arcade game — is a genuinely deep cut for a $19.99 set.

How to value a Mario minifigure

Nobody can quote you a price on a figure that doesn’t exist yet, and you should be skeptical of anyone who tries. What you can do right now, months before launch, is read the lineup the way a dealer reads it. Minifigure values aren’t random. They come out of a handful of mechanics that repeat with every wave LEGO releases.

1. Count the sets each figure appears in

This is the single most predictive thing you can check, and it costs nothing. A figure that appears in one box has exactly one source of supply. A figure that appears in four boxes has four, and every one of them floods the market with more copies as people part sets out.

Run the count on this wave and it sorts itself instantly:

  • One set only: Luigi, Fire Luigi, Bowser Jr., Yoshi, Toad, Peach, Fire Mario, Bowser, Sidestepper, the Cow
  • Two sets: Koopa Troopa
  • Four sets: Goomba, and Mario (across his outfit variants)

Goomba will be the cheapest Mario-universe figure on the secondary market, because LEGO is about to produce an enormous number of them. Standard Mario is close behind. That’s not a bad thing — it means a kid can get a Mario minifig for pocket change — but don’t pay a premium for either.

2. Check the price of the cheapest set that contains it

Exclusivity alone isn’t enough. You also have to know what it costs to get one. That’s the “price gate,” and it’s why the same character can be cheap in one wave and expensive in another.

Princess Peach, Fire Mario and Bowser are all locked behind 72060 at $99.99. If you want Peach and you don’t want to spend a hundred dollars on a castle, your only route is buying her as a single. Multiply that by every Peach fan who feels the same way, against a supply capped by however many $99.99 castles LEGO sells, and you get the textbook setup for the strongest single-figure market in a wave.

Compare Luigi, gated behind a $29.99 set. Plenty of people will just buy the box. Low gate, cheaper single.

3. Pay up for a first appearance

The first minifigure version of a famous character carries a premium later versions rarely match. It’s the one collectors point at years later, the one that anchors a “complete the character” display, and the one with the smallest production window before LEGO inevitably revises the design.

Every figure in this wave is a first appearance. That’s unusual, and it will never be true of Mario again. By the third or fourth wave there will be several Marios and nobody will care much about the last one. If you want the historically interesting versions, this is the wave.

4. Unique molds and big figs play by different rules

A minifigure assembled from existing parts with a new printed face is cheap to reproduce and easy for LEGO to reuse. A figure with brand-new molds — Yoshi’s head, Bowser’s entire body, Goomba, the Koopa shell — represents real tooling investment, which means LEGO commits to the design and sticks with it.

Big figs are their own market. They’re display pieces, almost always one per set, rarely reissued, and villains reliably outperform heroes. Bowser is the most recognizable villain in gaming, he’s an entirely new figure type, and he’s in the $99.99 box. Watch him.

5. Accessories and completeness are most of the difference

This is where people quietly lose money. A minifigure’s market price assumes it is complete — every accessory, every hat, every printed element that shipped with it. Mario’s cap, Peach’s crown, a Koopa shell, a power-up element: lose one and you’re not selling a Peach, you’re selling a partial Peach at a steep discount.

Condition on the printed parts matters just as much. Rubbed face printing, scratched torsos and cracked neck posts all take real money off, and printing wear is the one thing you cannot undo. Our guide to cleaning used LEGO covers how to freshen figures without destroying the print — which, if you take one thing from this section, is the trap to avoid.

6. Timing: the launch flood, then the retirement squeeze

Every wave follows the same price curve, and knowing it is worth more than any individual prediction:

  • Launch (January 2027): singles are scarce and overpriced — sellers are parting out the first boxes and buyers are impatient. A bad time to buy.
  • Months two through six: supply floods in as thousands of sets get parted out, and prices bottom. This is when you buy the figures you want.
  • Retirement (typically 18–30 months in): boxes stop shipping, part-outs dry up, and the exclusives start climbing. This is when you sell.

We covered that end of the curve in the sets retiring in 2026 — the squeeze is real, and it’s predictable.

If all you want is one Mario on a shelf, 72052 at $9.99 is unbeatable and you can ignore everything else on this list.

The watch list

Putting the six rules together, here’s how we’d rank the wave by likelihood of holding value. To be clear, this is a framework applied to announced facts — not a price guarantee. The market decides in 2027, not us.

  • Bowser (72060) — new figure type, iconic villain, single set, highest price gate. The strongest case in the wave.
  • Princess Peach (72060) — huge character demand, one source, $99.99 gate. Likely the most-searched single.
  • Fire Mario (72060) — the desirable Mario variant, locked in the expensive box while plain Mario floods in from four sets.
  • Yoshi (72057) — beloved, new molds, one set, mid-range gate.
  • Toad (72058) — one set, and it’s the $59.99 box. Underrated.
  • The Cow (72058) — the wildcard. Meme-famous, brick-built, single set. This is the kind of figure that surprises people.
  • Sidestepper (72055) — deep-cut arcade character in a cheap set. Low gate, but nostalgia is a real multiplier.
  • Luigi / Fire Luigi / Bowser Jr. — exclusive, but cheaply gated at $29.99. Steady, not spectacular.
  • Mario, Goomba, Koopa Troopa — multi-set figures. Cheap, and staying cheap.

What to do between now and January

You can’t buy these yet, so use the runway:

  1. Decide whether you’re a builder or a figure collector. Builders buy boxes. Figure collectors should wait for singles — almost always cheaper than buying a whole set to harvest one figure.
  2. Write down the two or three figures you actually want. A wave like this is designed to make you buy all eight boxes. You don’t need all eight boxes.
  3. Don’t buy singles in January. Mark spring 2027 instead, when the part-outs land and prices bottom out.
  4. Get your existing figures in order. If you plan to trade up, complete figures with intact printing are what actually sell. Our minifigure collecting guide and our rundown of the most valuable LEGO minifigures are the two worth reading first.
