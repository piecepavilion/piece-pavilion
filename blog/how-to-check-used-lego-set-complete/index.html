How to Check a Used LEGO Set Is Actually Complete Before You Buy It

Piece Pavilion · November 3, 2026 · 3 min read

A used set that says "complete" in the listing is a claim, not a fact. The only way to know is to check the pieces against the inventory, and you can do most of that before you pay. Here is the order we use when we check sets and lots at Piece Pavilion.

Start with the set number and the inventory

Every LEGO set has a number printed on the box and on the first page of the instruction booklet. Use that number to pull the official parts list from BrickLink. The inventory lists every part by shape and color, including the small ones that go missing first. If a seller cannot tell you the set number, assume you are buying an unverified pile.

Ask what "complete" actually means

Sellers use the word three different ways. Some mean every brick is present. Some mean every brick plus the minifigures. Fewer mean bricks, minifigures, stickers and instructions. Ask which one it is, and ask whether the seller counted against the inventory or is going from memory. A seller who counted will say so and will tell you what is missing.

Know which pieces go missing first

In our experience, the same categories come up short again and again:

  • Minifigure accessories. Weapons, hats, hair and tools are tiny and easy to lose.
  • Printed and stickered parts. Check that stickers were applied cleanly or are still on the sheet.
  • Single-use specialty parts. Windscreens, molded pieces and one-of-a-kind elements are expensive to replace.
  • Small connectors. Pins, axles and clips are used in bulk and lost in bulk.

Check the condition, not just the count

A complete set in poor shape is not a bargain. Look at photos for yellowed white bricks, which come from sunlight and heat, and for cracked or stress-marked plastic. Our guide on storing LEGO long-term explains why those problems happen. Ask for photos in natural light of the white and light gray parts, the printed pieces and the minifigure faces.

Decide whether missing parts are a dealbreaker

A set missing a handful of common parts is usually a fine buy, because you can order those pieces individually. A set missing rare parts or minifigures is a different deal. Price the replacements before you commit. If the replacements cost close to the gap between the used price and a complete one, buy the complete one. Our guide on whether used LEGO is worth buying covers where the savings come from.

The takeaway

Get the set number, ask what "complete" means, and price any missing parts before you buy. Ten minutes of checking beats a half-finished build.
