Technic Pin Colors Explained: Which Pin Grips and Which One Spins

Piece Pavilion · November 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Technic pins all look like the same little plug until you try to swap one for another. Then a joint that should spin freely won't move, or a joint that should hold a load flops around. The difference comes down to one feature: friction ridges. Once you know the color code, you can sort a bin of pins in a few minutes.

Friction versus frictionless

A friction pin has small ridges along its shaft. They grip the inside of a Technic hole, so the pin stays put and the joint holds its pose. A frictionless pin is smooth. It spins freely in the hole, which makes it the right choice for hinges, steering links and anything that has to swing. LEGO has used color to signal which is which for decades, and although there are exceptions, the pattern is reliable enough to sort by.

The pins you'll see most

  • Black pin with friction ridges (part 2780). The workhorse. It sits in a huge number of Technic sets, it's cheap in bulk, and it's the first one to reach for when a build needs a rigid joint.
  • Light bluish gray pin without ridges (part 3673). The classic frictionless pin. Older examples are light gray, from before the 2004 color change.
  • Blue 3L pin with friction and stop bush (part 6558). Longer, so it can pass through two beams and a connector. The stop bush keeps it from sliding all the way through.
  • Blue axle pin (part 43093). A pin on one end and an axle on the other, so it connects a pin hole to an axle hole. It carries friction ridges.
  • Tan 3L pin without ridges (part 32556) and tan axle pin (part 3749). Tan generally means a smooth, free-spinning part, which is handy for moving linkages.

Why this matters when you're buying used

If you're filling out a Technic build from a bulk lot, don't count pins as one generic item. A lot that's mostly black friction pins won't help much if your model needs a dozen free-spinning ones. Check the listing photos for color before you assume. Also check for worn ridges on old black pins. A pin that's been pushed in and pulled out hundreds of times can lose its grip, and that's worth knowing before you build a load-bearing joint with it.

A quick sorting habit

Keep friction pins and frictionless pins in separate compartments, even if you don't separate anything else. It's the single most useful sort for Technic work, because the cost of picking the wrong one only shows up after you've finished the section and gone to test it.

Our take

If you only learn one Technic color rule, make it this one: black grips, gray and tan glide, and blue is usually the long or axle-ended pin with friction. Check an exact part number on its BrickLink catalog page before you pay for a large quantity, since LEGO does occasionally release a pin in a new color or with a different function.
