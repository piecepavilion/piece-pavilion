How to Clean Used LEGO Safely (Without Damaging It)

Piece Pavilion · June 8, 2026

Used LEGO is one of the smartest ways to build a collection — you get retired sets, hard-to-find parts, and great value (we make the full case in Is Used LEGO Worth Buying?). The one catch with secondhand bricks is that they sometimes arrive dusty, sticky, or straight out of someone's attic. The good news: LEGO is easy to clean, as long as you do it the right way.

Do it the wrong way, though — too-hot water, the dishwasher, harsh chemicals — and you can warp pieces, fade printing, or cloud clear parts permanently. Here's the safe method.

What You'll Need

  • A bowl or sink of lukewarm water
  • A few drops of mild dish soap (nothing with bleach or strong solvents)
  • A soft brush — an old toothbrush is perfect for grime in the studs
  • A colander or mesh laundry bag (handy for rinsing lots of small parts)
  • Clean towels for air-drying

1. The Basic Hand-Wash Method

For the vast majority of standard bricks and plates, this is all you need:

  • Use lukewarm water — never hot. Keep it below about 104°F (40°C). LEGO is made of ABS plastic, and heat is its enemy: hot water can warp pieces and ruin the precise "clutch" that makes bricks snap together.
  • Add a little mild dish soap and let the pieces soak for 10–15 minutes to loosen dirt.
  • Gently scrub stubborn spots with the soft brush, paying attention to the studs and tubes where dust collects.
  • Rinse thoroughly in clean lukewarm water — a colander or mesh bag makes rinsing a big batch easy.

2. What NOT to Do

Most "ruined LEGO" stories come down to one of these mistakes. Avoid them all:

  • Don't use the dishwasher. The heat and intense water jets warp and discolor pieces, and small parts get blasted everywhere. It's the single most common way people destroy a batch of bricks.
  • Don't use hot water. Worth repeating — warm-to-the-touch is the absolute ceiling.
  • Don't use bleach, alcohol, or harsh cleaners. They can discolor plastic and strip the printing off minifigures and decorated parts.
  • Don't scrub printed surfaces with abrasives. No scouring pads or baking-soda scrubs on faces, torsos, or decorated tiles.

3. Cleaning Minifigures and Printed Parts

Minifigures and decorated pieces need a gentler touch, because the printing can wear off (a big factor in a figure's value — see our minifigure collecting guide). Wash them by hand in mild soapy water, but don't scrub the printed areas — just wipe gently with your fingers or a soft cloth. Take minifigures apart (head, torso, legs, accessories) so you can clean the joints without forcing anything.

4. Be Careful With Special Pieces

  • Electronics — Never submerge motors, battery boxes, lights, or Power Functions / Powered Up components. Wipe them down with a barely-damp cloth instead.
  • Stickered parts — Soaking lifts stickers and ruins them. Spot-clean around stickers with a damp cloth rather than submerging.
  • Clear / transparent pieces — These scratch and cloud more easily, so handle them gently and never use anything abrasive.
  • Large or sealed elements — Anything that can trap water inside (like some big specialty parts) should be shaken out and given extra drying time.

5. Dry Them Completely

Drying matters as much as washing — trapped moisture leads to mildew and water spots.

  • Air-dry only. Spread the pieces in a single layer on a clean towel, out of direct sunlight.
  • No heat. Skip the hairdryer, oven, radiator, and sunny windowsill — all of them can warp or fade the plastic.
  • Give it time. Studs and tubes hold water, so let everything dry for a day or two. Make sure pieces are completely dry before bagging or boxing them, or you'll trap humidity and risk mildew.

6. A Word on Yellowed LEGO

Old white and light-gray bricks often turn yellow over years of sun exposure — that's the plastic itself changing, not dirt, so washing won't fix it. There's an advanced restoration method (often called "retrobrighting") using UV light and hydrogen peroxide, but it's finicky and can damage pieces if done wrong. For most collectors it's not worth the risk; if a piece's color matters to you, it's usually easier to simply replace it.
