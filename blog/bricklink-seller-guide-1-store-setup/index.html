BrickLink Guide #1: Setting Your Store Up for Volume

Piece Pavilion · November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

A few times a week we post a short guide for other BrickLink sellers — the small, specific things that have actually helped us move inventory. New guides land every Monday, Wednesday and Friday. If one of them helps you, that is a win.

This first guide is the one we think about most: setting your store up so inventory moves without you babysitting it.

1. Tag every lot with a real bin location, in Remarks

The biggest difference between a store that packs orders in ten minutes and a store that packs them in forty is whether every lot has a bin location in its Remarks field. BrickLink prints Remarks on the pick sheet, and your eyes stop looking at the part number as soon as you see something like A3-R or B12-top. Pick a scheme that fits your space (aisle-shelf-position works well) and stick to it. If a lot has no bin, it does not exist on your pick sheet.

The mistake I made starting out: I did not take labeling seriously at first. I thought I would remember where things lived, or that I could backfill the bin codes later. Years in, I am still fixing lots from the early days that have no bin or have the wrong bin, and every one of those is an order that takes me five minutes longer than it should. If you are starting a store right now, set up your bin scheme on day one and tag every single lot before it goes live. Future you will thank you.

2. Start with almost no minimum order and no lot limit

When you are new on BrickLink, every barrier you put in front of a buyer costs you an order you were going to get. A minimum purchase amount scares off the buyer who just wants one 50 cent piece to finish a MOC — and that same buyer is the one who comes back next week for twenty dollars of parts because you were easy to work with the first time. A lot-count minimum ("must buy at least 5 lots") does the same thing. BrickLink's search filter lets buyers hide stores with high minimums, so a $20 minimum literally takes you out of search results for anyone shopping small.

I will say this though: you probably do not want to leave the minimum at a true zero either. BrickLink lets a buyer place a one-penny order, and once the PayPal fee comes out you are losing money just processing it. A sensible floor is somewhere between $0.50 and $1.50 — low enough that nobody shopping legitimately gets turned away, high enough that you are not losing a dollar on a pure nuisance order. We ran ours at $1.00 for a long time and it never cost us a sale.

The lot-count limit is the one to leave truly at zero. There is no cost to you if a buyer wants a single lot, so there is no reason to require five. Every lot limit I have ever had on turned out to be a mistake — the buyer who gets blocked by it is almost always a first-time customer, and first-time customers are the whole game at a new store.

Also, make sure your Store Terms match. If your dashboard says "Minimum: $1.00" but your Terms say "We prefer orders over $20" buyers notice and avoid you. Keep the two in sync, and update them together whenever you change either number.

Tighten later, once you are drowning in orders and your repeat-buyer rate is high enough that a few lost first-timers do not sting. At the start, volume and feedback count matter more than squeezing an extra dollar out of each order.

3. Keep your shipping cost as low as you honestly can

This is the same idea as the no-minimum rule, applied to shipping. Low shipping wins orders. Yes, your average order gets smaller when shipping is cheap — a lot of buyers will add just the one or two parts they need instead of padding the cart to justify the postage. But you get many more of those smaller orders, and the smaller orders are the ones that build repeat buyers and feedback.

Not every store can do rock-bottom shipping — if you are selling heavy sets or shipping internationally, there is a floor. But for parts and minifigs in the US, keep your padded-envelope rate honest and your Priority Mail cutoff reasonable. A buyer who sees $8 shipping on a $3 part will close the tab. A buyer who sees $1 shipping on that same part will add two more lots and come back next month.

4. Write your Store Terms so they handle the five awkward situations

Most buyers never read Store Terms. The ones who read them do it after something awkward happens, and that is when your terms earn their keep. Yours should answer, in two or three lines each, these five situations:

  • Combined shipping on repeat orders in a short window.
  • What happens if a lot is miscounted or missing on your end — refund, replace or credit.
  • How you handle a buyer-requested cancellation before shipping.
  • Non-paying buyer policy and the window before you cancel.
  • International shipping rules, including the countries you do not ship to.

Write once. Reference it every time a buyer asks. If a situation keeps coming up that is not in Terms, add it — your Terms should grow with your store.

5. Anchor your pricing to the six-month average, not the peak

Re-price your inventory weekly against the BrickLink price guide's last six-month new-condition average, not the all-time peak. A figure that printed $17 in September can trade $14 in November, and the sellers who leave the tag at $17 just stop moving inventory. The six-month average is close enough to today's reality that your prices stay competitive, and far enough back that you are not chasing weekly noise.

If you sell a lot of used parts, the same rule applies with the used-condition six-month average. If you only have a handful of listings in a given category, pick the ten most expensive and re-price those first; the long tail of cheap bricks almost never moves the needle.
